The Language Learning Guide

Most people who give up on a language do so because their plan was unrealistic, not because they lacked talent. Here is a plan built to last.

Set a goal you can measure

“Become fluent” is hard to track. “Hold a ten-minute conversation about my work by June” is clear. Tie your goal to a real situation, a level on the CEFR scale and a date.

Understand the levels

LevelYou can…
A1Introduce yourself and use simple phrases
A2Handle routine tasks such as shopping and directions
B1Cope with most travel situations and describe experiences
B2Talk comfortably with native speakers on familiar topics
C1Use the language flexibly for work and study
C2Understand virtually everything with ease

How long it really takes

Published estimates for English speakers range from roughly 600–750 class hours for languages closely related to English to around 2,200 hours for languages such as Japanese or Arabic, to reach a strong working level. Self-study hours vary widely, so treat any figure as a rough guide. The Language Level Estimator gives a personal estimate.

A balanced weekly plan

  • Daily: 20–30 minutes of vocabulary review and listening.
  • Three times a week: a grammar point with practice sentences.
  • Twice a week: speaking practice, alone or with a partner.
  • Weekly: read one short text or watch one episode with target-language subtitles.

Use the language early

Speak from the first week, even if it is only a few sentences. Mistakes are part of the process, and using new words quickly is one of the best ways to remember them.

When progress feels slow

The intermediate plateau is normal: you know enough to notice how much you don’t know. Change the input (a new podcast, a new topic), track hours rather than feelings, and look back at what you couldn’t understand three months ago.
